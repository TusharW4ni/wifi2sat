GEOM-DECORRELATION · κ derivation
A derivation, built from scratch

How long does a gesture stay legible
as the sky drifts overhead? κ

A hand near a fixed GPS antenna bends the satellite signal. Do the same gesture a few minutes later and the satellites have quietly moved. This lecture derives, one honest step at a time, exactly how fast that motion erases the gesture, and why some gestures shrug it off while others fall apart. The whole answer collapses into a single number.

00 The one-paragraph map

Where we're going

We start with a physical fact (a hand shifts the phase of a satellite signal) and end with a formula for the usable time window of a gesture. The road has five landmarks, and every symbol you meet lives in the Σ Symbols panel (top-right) so you never have to scroll back. Hover any colored symbol in the text or equations to light up its siblings.

  • ê Geometry: where the satellites are, and how that turns hand-motion into phase.
  • s The clean signal: strip away everything that isn't the gesture.
  • M Gesture shape: compress a 3-D path into one matrix.
  • κ The curvature: one number that says how fast reproducibility dies.
  • δt The window: turn κ into seconds and minutes.
The whole idea in one breath A gesture is a little 3-D shape. Each satellite sees a flattened shadow of that shape. As satellites drift, the direction of the shadow rotates. If the shape is a straight line, its shadow only changes length, which is harmless. If the shape is a 2-D or 3-D blob, its shadow changes form, and the signal stops matching itself. κ measures exactly that.
01 The physical picture

A hand is a moving mirror

A GNSS satellite is ~20,000 km away, so its radio wave arrives as essentially parallel rays. The antenna is bolted down: it never moves. When your hand comes near, the wave can also bounce off your skin and reach the antenna by a slightly longer, second path. This bonus path is multipath, and it interferes with the direct wave.

The carrier wave is a pure sinusoid with wavelength λ ≈ 19 cm (GPS L1). The receiver measures the wave's phase to a fraction of a millimeter. So even a small change in the reflected path length shows up as a measurable phase wiggle. Move your hand, change the extra path, wiggle the phase. That wiggle is the gesture.

Here's the key simplification. Because the incoming rays are parallel, when your hand moves by a displacement d, the extra path length changes by the projection of that motion onto the line-of-sight direction ê. Motion along the ray matters fully; motion across the ray barely matters. Drag the hand below and watch it.

Why projection? For far-away parallel rays, the extra distance the reflected wave travels when the mirror moves by d is ê·d, a dot product. Dot product = "how much of this points along that." A displacement perpendicular to the ray contributes zero to first order. Hold that thought; it becomes the entire model in the next step.
02 The signal model

Write down what the receiver sees

Putting the projection idea into a formula: the phase perturbation the receiver records on satellite i is

φi(t) ≈ 2πλ êi·d(t) + c(t) + ρi(t) + noise measured phase = gesture term + receiver clock + range trend + noise

Read it left to right:

  • 2πλ êi·d(t): the gesture. The 2π⁄λ converts a length into radians of phase (one wavelength = one full turn = 2π). This is the only term we want.
  • c(t): the receiver clock error. The receiver's cheap clock drifts, and this adds the same phase to every satellite at once.
  • ρi(t): the range trend. The satellite is genuinely moving through the sky, so the true distance changes slowly and smoothly over our few-second window.
  • noise: thermal and tracking noise.

Three of these four are nuisances. The next step kills them both cleanly, and the trick is almost embarrassingly simple.

03 Peel away the nuisances

Two subtractions and the gesture pops out

Single-differencing kills the clock

The clock error c(t) is identical on every satellite (it's the receiver's clock, not the satellite's). So pick one satellite as a reference and subtract its phase from everyone else's. The clock term is common, so it cancels, exactly, for free. This is single-differencing.

Detrending kills the range trend

The range trend ρi(t) is smooth and slow: over a 3-second window it looks like a gentle parabola. A gesture is a fast wiggle on top. Fit a low-order polynomial (2nd or 3rd order) to each satellite's window and subtract it. The slow trend leaves; the fast gesture stays. This is detrending.

After both subtractions, the four-term equation becomes gloriously short:

si(t) = gi·d(t) gi = 2πλ (êi − êref) clean gesture signal = differential sensitivity vector · hand displacement

That's the workhorse of the whole project. The clean signal on satellite i is a simple dot product: a fixed sensitivity vector gi dotted with the moving hand d(t). Everything about the sky is baked into gi: it's the difference of two line-of-sight vectors, scaled by 2π⁄λ. Everything about the gesture is in d(t).

Mental model gi is a fixed arrow in space: a "camera direction." si(t) is what that one camera sees of the gesture: its 1-D shadow. Different satellites are cameras pointing from different angles, each recording a different shadow of the same hand motion.
04 Do it again, later

How similar are two repetitions?

Now the real question. Perform the same gesture d(t) at time A, then again at time B, Δt later. In between, the satellites have crept across the sky, so each sensitivity vector rotates: gA → gB. Do the two recorded signals still look alike?

We measure "alike" with the correlation r: the normalized overlap of the two waveforms. r = 1 means identical shape; r = 0 means unrelated. Crucially, correlation ignores overall size: a signal and a scaled copy of it correlate at r = 1. Remember that; it's the punchline later.

r = ∫ sA sB dt √∫ sA2 dt · √∫ sB2 dt zero-lag correlation over the gesture window

Substitute s = g·d and something beautiful happens. Look at a single overlap integral:

∫ (gA·d)(gB·d) dt = gA⊤ (∫ d d⊤ dt) gB = gA⊤ M gB

The g-vectors are constants, so they slide out of the time integral, and everything about the gesture collects into one 3×3 matrix in the middle. Meet the star of the show:

M = ∫0Tg d(t) d(t)⊤ dt the structure matrix: the gesture's shape, frozen into one 3×3 matrix

What is M, really?

M is the scatter matrix of the trajectory, statistically its covariance (up to scale). It answers "which directions did the hand actually explore, and how much?" Picture it as an ellipsoid: a long thin cigar if the gesture is basically a straight line; a round ball if the gesture roams in all directions. Its eigenvectors are the gesture's principal axes; its eigenvalues are how far it traveled along each.

With M in hand, the messy correlation of waveforms becomes a clean piece of geometry: the generalized cosine between the two sensitivity vectors, measured in the metric M:

r = gA⊤ M gB √gA⊤ M gA · √gB⊤ M gB
The load-bearing sentence Ordinary cosine of two vectors is gA·gB / (|gA||gB|). This is the same thing, but distances and angles are warped by M: the gesture's shape decides which directions "count." All the geometry drift is in gA→gB; all the gesture shape is in M. The rest of the derivation is just watching this cosine fall as the sky rotates gA into gB.
05 The small-drift expansion

Squeeze out the one number: κ

Over a few minutes the sky barely moves, so gB is only a little different from gA. Write the difference as Δg and expand the cosine for small drift. This is the mathematical heart of the whole project, so we'll go one line at a time. Toggle the algebra if you want every step.

Write gB = gA + Δg, and name the three quadratic pieces that appear:

a = g⊤Mg b = g⊤MΔg c = Δg⊤MΔg a is order 1 · b is order Δθ · c is order Δθ²

The numerator is a + b. The denominator's second factor is √(a + 2b + c). So, dividing top and bottom by a and writing β=b/a, γ=c/a:

r = a+b√a √a+2b+c = 1+β√1+2β+γ

Taylor-expand the square root to second order (√(1+x) ≈ 1 + x/2 − x²/8), then multiply out and keep terms up to Δθ². The order-Δθ terms cancel exactly, leaving:

r ≈ 1 − 12 (γ − β2) ⟹ 1 − r ≈ 12 c a − b2a2

Last move: the drift is a rotation by a tiny angle Δθ, so Δg = Δθ w, where w = dg/dθ is the fixed direction the sensitivity vector drifts in. Plug that in and the Δθ factors pull out front:

r ≈ 1 − 12 κ Δθ2 κ = (w⊤Mw)(g⊤Mg) − (g⊤Mw)2 (g⊤Mg)2 decorrelation is quadratic in drift, and κ is the curvature of that parabola

That's it: the entire theoretical payoff. Correlation starts at 1 and falls off quadratically (like a parabola, not a straight line) as the sky drifts, and the steepness of that parabola is a single dimensionless number κ built only from the gesture shape M and the geometry vectors g, w.

06 Two facts about κ

It's never negative, and it can be exactly zero

κ ≥ 0 always (correlation can only get worse)

Look back at the numerator of κ: (w⊤Mw)(g⊤Mg) − (g⊤Mw)². That's exactly the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the M-metric: for any two vectors, (their M-inner-product)² ≤ (each M-length)². So the numerator is ≥ 0, and κ ≥ 0. Drift can never increase the correlation, which matches intuition: the sky wandering off can only make a repeat look less like the original, never more.

κ = 0 exactly when the gesture is one straight line

This is the whole headline, and it drops out in three lines. If the gesture moves along a single fixed direction n̂, then d(t) = f(t) n̂ and the structure matrix collapses to rank 1:

M = (∫ f2 dt) n̂ n̂⊤ a single-axis gesture: the cigar collapsed to a needle

The magic property of a rank-1 matrix: M times anything points along n̂. So Mg and Mw are parallel. Feed that into κ's numerator and the two terms become identical, so they subtract to exactly zero:

κ1D ∝ (n̂·w)2(n̂·g)2 − (n̂·g)2(n̂·w)2 = 0
Why this makes physical sense A straight-line gesture is a single camera-shadow: the satellite only ever sees the scalar n̂·g times the fixed waveform f(t). When the sky drifts, that scalar changes, but it only rescales the amplitude of f(t); the shape is untouched. And correlation ignores amplitude (Step 04). So r stays pinned at 1. A round gesture instead paints a 2-D shadow whose shape morphs as g rotates, and that's what correlation punishes. Dimension of the gesture, not its size, sets the window.

Confirm it yourself in Fig 3: drag the shape slider all the way to "thin." As the ellipse becomes a needle, κ crashes toward 0 and the correlation curve flattens into a horizontal line, no matter how far you push the drift slider.

07 The two probe gestures

Push and star: the κ extremes

The experiment uses two gestures chosen to sit at opposite ends of the κ scale, so everything else falls in between. Their structure matrices were measured from real recovered data; the eigenvalues below are the (normalized) spreads of M along its three axes.

gesturecharacterM eigenvalues (norm.)κwindow
pushnear single-axis[1, 0.009, 0.008]≈ 0wide (geometry-proof)
stargenuinely multi-axis[1, 0.34, 0.28]> 0tight (the binding limit)

Push is almost pure rank-1: its second and third eigenvalues are under 1% of the first, so it's a needle, and κ ≈ 0. Star spreads a third of its energy into the off-axes, so M is fat, κ is finite, and the signal decorrelates quadratically. The ratio of their windows is set entirely by √(κstar/κpush): pure geometry of the two shapes.

The prediction this makes If the theory is right, a classifier trained at one time-of-day and tested later should fail gesture-by-gesture, not uniformly: push should survive geometry drift, star should collapse first. That gesture-shape-dependent failure is the signature the experiment hunts for.
08 From κ to a stopwatch

The usable window δtmax

κ is dimensionless. To get a time, we need two more facts: how much correlation we're willing to lose, and how fast the sky actually rotates.

Pick a minimum acceptable correlation rmin. Set the parabola equal to it and solve for the drift you can tolerate:

rmin = 1 − 12κ Δθmax2 ⟹ Δθmax = √2(1 − rmin)κ

The sensitivity vector rotates at a steady rate ωLOS ≈ 0.45°/min, and here's the key point: this is not a knob you turn. It's fixed by how fast the satellites orbit, which you can't change. What you can change is which constellation you track, because different orbits move at different speeds. Since drift = rate × time, Δθ = ωLOS δt, and the window is:

δtmax = 1ωLOS √2(1 − rmin)κ the headline formula: window shrinks like 1/√κ
How do we actually know ωLOS ≈ 0.45°/min? (and is it the same for every satellite?)

Two independent ways: one from physics, one from the data itself.

1 · From orbital mechanics (the sanity check). The sky moves because the satellites move. A GPS satellite completes one orbit in about 11 h 58 m (it laps the Earth twice per day). Its angular speed around Earth's center is therefore

n = 360°11.97 h × 60 ≈ 0.50°/min.

That orbital rate is the dominant term. The line-of-sight rate seen from the ground is the same order, nudged by range and elevation (a satellite overhead sweeps a bit faster than one near the horizon), which is why it lands near 0.45–0.50 rather than exactly 0.50.

2 · Measured straight from our captures (the real source of 0.45). Every capture stores each satellite's line-of-sight unit vector êi per epoch (the los_enu field). So you don't have to model anything: just take the angle between ê now and ê a minute later and divide by the minute. Across our satellites and elevations that clusters at 0.42–0.52°/min, hence the working figure ≈ 0.45. It barely depends on elevation because the rate is set by orbital motion, not by where the satellite sits in the sky.

Is it constellation-specific? Yes: orbit altitude sets the speed.

constellationorbit typeperiod≈ ωLOS
GPSMEO (~20,200 km)11 h 58 m0.50°/min
BeiDou-MEOMEO (~21,500 km)12 h 53 m0.47°/min
BeiDou GEO / IGSOgeo-synchronous~24 h≈ 0 (~0.03)

GPS and BeiDou-MEO are close (both are medium-Earth-orbit, ~0.45–0.50°/min), which is why the project lumps them together. But BeiDou also flies geostationary (GEO) and inclined-geosync (IGSO) birds that hang almost still over the ground: their ωLOS ≈ 0, so their geometric window is effectively unlimited. Same orbital difference is why the geometries repeat on different clocks: GPS every 1 sidereal day, BeiDou-MEO every 7 (Step 10). Bottom line: ωLOS is physics, but it's per-constellation physics, so in the calculator below you pick a constellation, you don't dial a rate.

09 The catch

Hands aren't machines: the floor α

Everything so far assumed the gesture is identical both times. It isn't. Even at zero geometry drift, two free-hand repetitions don't perfectly match: you can't move your hand the exact same way twice. So the correlation doesn't start at 1; it starts at a ceiling α < 1, the reproducibility floor. A better model multiplies the two effects:

r(δt) ≈ α (1 − 12κ ωLOS2 δt2) repeatability × geometry falloff

Here's why this matters so much. To measure the geometric window, the parabola dip has to be visible above the reproducibility noise. But measured free-hand α ≈ 0.6: well below the ~0.85 physical noise ceiling. The hand-sloppiness swamps the gentle geometry falloff, so κ is buried. The honest result On free-hand data this is where the thesis stalls: reproducibility (α≈0.6) is too low to isolate the geometric κ, the recoverable signal turns out to be mostly amplitude rather than phase-shape, and it doesn't transfer across days. The theory is clean; demonstrating it needs mechanically reproduced gestures: the derivation tells you precisely why. See the project's FINDINGS write-up for the full post-mortem.

Does the mechanical window transfer to your hand?

Here's the natural worry, and it's the right one to have: the real product is free-hand. So if the rig measures a window Z, does that Z still hold when a person does the gesture? Split the falloff back into its two independent parts and the answer drops out cleanly.

The one decomposition to remember κ is the geometry clock: it depends only on the gesture shape and the sky, never on who performs it. α is the starting line: it depends only on the performer. A machine starts at α ≈ 0.85; a free hand starts at α ≈ 0.60, and jittery.

So the rate of geometric decorrelation, measured as a fraction of wherever it starts, is identical for hand and machine: r(δt) / α = 1 − ½κωLOS2δt2. The geometry clock ticks at the same speed for everyone. The hand just starts the race further back, and shaking.

The catch appears the instant you demand an absolute quality bar rmin. Carry α all the way through the window formula and it now sits inside:

δtmax = 1ωLOS √2(1 − rmin / α)κ the same window formula: now honest about the starting line. If rmin > α, there is no real solution at all.

Set rmin = 0.8 and give it a hand's α ≈ 0.6: then rmin/α > 1, the square root goes imaginary, and the window is undefined: the hand fails the bar at zero drift, before the sky moves at all. For a bare-hand template match, reproducibility, not geometry, is the binding limit, so Z(hand) ≤ Z(machine), and often far less. Drag the bar below: watch the machine earn a window while the hand can't even line up at the start.

And the star gets hit twice. It is the highest-κ gesture, the tightest geometric window, and the hardest to reproduce, since multi-axis motion gives your hand more ways to wander (lower α). It decorrelates faster from the sky and from your own hand at once. Toggle the panel to push and watch both windows stretch out; that's the low-κ, easy-to-repeat end.

So what is the mechanical Z actually for? It is not the product's operating window. The free-hand product never leans on signal correlation: it trains a classifier across geometry (that's the eventual goal). What the rig buys you is the geometry budget: measured cleanly, with α pushed near 1 so it isn't buried, κ tells you the timescale on which the signal genuinely changes shape. That tells you how densely to sample geometry when you collect hand data, and which gestures (star) demand it most. You use a machine to read κ precisely because the hand can't, and then κ, being performer-independent, hands that budget straight to the free-hand system.

One honest wrinkle: a hand doesn't trace one fixed shape M: it draws a distribution of shapes, so what transfers is an effective κ averaged over that spread. Program the rig to trace a representative gesture and the budget carries over; let the hand drift into a different shape and you're measuring a different κ.

10 Measuring it directly

Ask the sky to repeat itself

You don't have to wait around for natural drift to trace out r(Δθ). Satellite geometry repeats: GPS returns to the same configuration one sidereal day later: 23h 56m 04s, about 3m 56s short of a solar day. So the same patch of sky you saw at noon today shows up at 11:56 tomorrow.

Tsid = 86164.09 s ≈ solar day − 3m 56s

The clean measurement is the repeat-offset sweep: record a reference gesture at time T, then revisit at T + Tsid + ε for a handful of small offsets ε. Each offset injects a known drift Δθ = ωLOS ε, so sweeping ε traces the decorrelation curve directly, and you check it against the predicted 1 − ½κΔθ². 11 The teach-back

Say it back in five sentences

If you can say these out loud without peeking, you own the derivation:

01 · SIGNAL

A hand near the antenna shifts each satellite's carrier phase by the projection of its motion onto the line of sight; strip the clock and range trend and the clean signal is just s = g·d.

02 · SHAPE

Repeat the gesture later and correlation becomes a cosine in the metric M: the gesture's 3×3 shape matrix carries all the shape, the vectors g carry all the sky.

03 · CURVATURE

For small drift, correlation falls like a parabola: r ≈ 1 − ½κΔθ², with one dimensionless curvature κ ≥ 0 built from M, g and the drift direction w.

04 · DIMENSION

A straight-line gesture makes M rank-1, so κ = 0: its shadow only rescales, and correlation ignores scale. A round gesture morphs its shadow, so κ > 0. Dimension, not size, sets the window.

05 · WINDOW

Turn κ into time: δt ∝ 1/√κ divided by the sky's rotation rate, but you only see it if the hand repeats well enough (α near the noise ceiling), which is why the measurement wants a machine.

The single sentence to leave people with How long a GNSS gesture stays recognizable is decided by its shape's dimensionality: a one-dimensional motion is geometry-proof (κ = 0), and every extra dimension you add shrinks the window like 1/√κ.
