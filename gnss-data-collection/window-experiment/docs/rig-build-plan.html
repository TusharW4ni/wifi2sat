Engineering plan · gesture-reproduction rig

Building the rig, in two phases

The diagnostic said the free-hand data can't measure the window because carrier-phase reproducibility (α) sits at the noise floor and geometry is confounded with time. A rig fixes both. But rather than build the versatile, RF-hostile workhorse first, we stage it: a cheap, minimal-metal Phase 1 rig asks the make-or-break question, and only if it passes do we build the Phase 2 workhorse that measures κ.

0 The logic of two phases

Answer the cheap question before building the expensive rig

Everything hinges on one unknown: does perfect mechanical repetition lift carrier-phase α off the noise floor at all? If yes, the geometry mechanism (κ) becomes measurable and the full rig is worth building. If no, the single-antenna phase channel simply doesn't carry usable gesture signal on this hardware, and that is a decisive, publishable negative you want to reach for a couple hundred dollars, not after building a gantry. So Phase 1 exists purely to pass or fail that gate.

1 The de-risk rig
Phase 1

Does mechanical repetition rescue the phase channel?

Goal: a single question, one gesture (push), maximum RF-cleanliness. We are not measuring κ yet; we are checking whether a flawlessly repeated motion produces a reproducible carrier-phase signal at all. The design rule: keep the drive far away, and let only the reflector plus a nonconductive rod approach the antenna.

Recommended option

A constant-speed crank (or Scotch-yoke) reproducer, with a pendulum as the same-day proof of life.

One gearmotor spins steadily at one end, a crank converts rotation into back-and-forth linear motion, and a long fiberglass/nylon rod pushes the reflector past the antenna. If you want a controlled ramp-and-hold profile with precise per-stroke onset instead of a sinusoid, substitute a leadscrew linear actuator plus stepper. It is barely more complex and repeats even better.

Why the crank

  • Minimal metal, minimal electronics. One motor, kept more than a meter away; near the antenna there is only a plastic rod and the reflector. This is exactly the RF-cleanliness the printer can't offer.
  • Repeatability is mechanically forced. A crank traces the same stroke every rotation with no control loop to drift.
  • It is periodic, which is the real gift. A steady drive gives hundreds of cycles you can fold and average (synchronous averaging / lock-in at the drive frequency). That pulls a weak periodic phase signal far up out of the noise, turning "is there any phase signal" from an unanswerable single-shot question into a high-SNR measurement. A human hand can never give you a stable frequency to lock into; a motor can.

Build (parts)

  • 12 V DC gearmotor, roughly 10 to 40 RPM (one push-pull cycle every few seconds, matching the gesture timescale).
  • 3-D-printed crank disk + connecting rod driving a low-friction slider (a drawer slide, or two smooth rods with linear bearings).
  • 30 to 50 cm RF-transparent pushrod: fiberglass tent-pole segment, nylon, or a wooden dowel (not carbon fiber, it conducts).
  • Reflector at the tip: start with a flat metal plate (the cleanest, most reproducible reflector).
  • A photogate + a small flag on the crank to mark each cycle's phase. This is the timing reference you fold on.
  • Motor driver + supply kept back with the electronics; ferrites on the leads.

Protocol & data

  • Run the crank continuously at a fixed cycle time for about 2 hours, logging RAWX + SFRBX at 10 Hz plus the photogate. That yields hundreds to thousands of cycles spread across roughly 50 degrees of natural geometry drift.
  • Controls (the programmable/periodic drive makes these trivial): (1) with reflector, (2) bare rod, no reflector, same motion = the machine's own artifact, (3) motor off, static = environment baseline.
  • Log C/N0 with the rig off, on-idle, and moving, to confirm the motor isn't desensing the receiver.

Analysis & the gate

Lock in (or fold) at the drive frequency per satellite to get a clean complex phase response per cycle. Then ask two things: is that response reproducibly above the noise null (an α proxy), and does its correlation with a reference cycle stay high (push should be geometry-robust, κ near zero)?

  • PASS: the phase response is clearly above noise and reproducible across cycles. Phase α lifts. Green-light Phase 2.
  • FAIL: even a perfectly repeated, lock-in-amplified push stays at the floor. The carrier-phase channel is unusable on this hardware. Stop, and write up the amplitude/proximity result. This is the cheap decisive negative the whole phase exists to find.

Cost: under $150. Time: days.

2 The workhorse
Phase 2

Measure κ per gesture, with geometry isolated

Goal (only if Phase 1 passes): measure the decorrelation curvature κ for the real gesture set, cleanly enough to test the theory's central prediction (push robust, star tight) and to produce a δtmax(rmin) curve. This needs arbitrary trajectories, interleaved gesture order, and geometry sampled two ways.

Recommended option

A modified CoreXY 3-D printer (or small moving-gantry CNC), with the heated bed removed and the reflector on a long RF-transparent standoff.

CoreXY over a bedslinger, because its motors are bolted to the frame (only a light toolhead moves near the reflector), whereas a bedslinger's X motor rides along and its bed-driven axis is an awkward metal plate. For full 3-D reflector motion, use a moving-gantry machine so the toolhead moves in all three axes; most of the gestures are planar, so a clean 2-axis plane is often enough.

Why it works, and the honest caveat

Trajectory, repeatability (about 50 µm), and homing are already solved by G-code and firmware, and being programmable buys the two fixes the diagnostic called for: interleaved gesture order for free (breaking the gesture-versus-time confound), and the with/without-reflector control to subtract the machine's own reflection. The caveat is real: a printer is a pile of moving metal and switching noise next to a sensitive antenna, so the work shifts from "make it move" (solved) to "keep its metal and EMI away from the antenna" (the shared build notes below).

Configuration

  • Remove the heated bed and glass (you are not printing); that deletes a big reflector and a heat source.
  • Klipper firmware, driven by a host script: home, dwell, trace the gesture (3 to 6 s), return, wait, repeat; toggle a GPIO pin at each onset to timestamp against the receiver.
  • Reflector on a 30 to 50 cm fiberglass/nylon standoff reaching toward the antenna, kept stiff so it doesn't flex.
  • Linear or battery power, ferrites, mainboard and PSU kept more than a meter from the antenna.

Protocol & data

  • Gestures: push and star at minimum (the κ extremes), ideally push, pushpull, triangle, m, star.
  • Interleave gesture order within each window (randomized) so the gesture label is decoupled from recording time.
  • Geometry, two ways: within-session, repeat the interleaved set every few minutes for about 2 hours to sweep roughly 50 degrees of drift; and the sidereal revisit, leave the rig and antenna bolted in place and re-run identically about 4 minutes earlier the next day (plus a few offsets ε) to hold geometry fixed while time changes.
  • Aim for roughly 100 to 150 executions per gesture for a solid r(Δθ) fit; every capture sees several satellites, each a separate geometry sample, which multiplies the data.
  • Same controls as Phase 1: bare-standoff run, static baseline, C/N0 desense check.

Output

  • κ per gesture, with the predicted push-versus-star ordering (or an honest null).
  • The δtmax(rmin) curve: the actual, measured window, at last.
  • A cross-day transfer result that is now attributable to geometry, because interleaving removed the time confound.

Cost: $150 to $500. Time: weeks.

3 Shared build notes

The parts that decide whether either rig works

Both rigs live or die on RF hygiene. The layout principle is identical: the antenna faces the sky, the reflector moves in its near field, and everything metallic or noisy stays far away on a nonconductive rod.

  • Reflector: start with a flat metal plate (cleanest, most reproducible, best chance of seeing the signal). Add a rigid tissue-mimicking phantom later to confirm the result holds for hand-like targets.
  • Arm: fiberglass, nylon, or wood, stiff enough that flex doesn't spoil repeatability. Never carbon fiber (it conducts).
  • Antenna: on its own rigid tripod, mechanically decoupled from the rig so motor vibration doesn't move it. A moving antenna breaks the whole model.
  • EMI: electronics more than a meter away, ferrites on cables, linear or battery power if possible. Then measure the desense (C/N0 off vs idle vs moving) before trusting anything.
  • Sidereal rigidity: bolt the rig and antenna together as one fixture and leave it between the sidereally-aligned sessions, so the only thing changing day to day is the sky.
  • Sync: a photogate on the crank (Phase 1) or a GPIO onset pin per stroke (Phase 2), logged beside the RAWX stream.

The plan in one line

Build a $150 dumb crank first and ask whether a perfectly repeated push lifts carrier-phase α off the floor. If it does, a modified CoreXY printer measures κ per gesture with interleaving and the sidereal revisit, and finally gives you a measured window. If it doesn't, you have saved yourself the gantry and earned an honest negative.
